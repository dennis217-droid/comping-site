About Comping

Last updated: 2026-08-08

Why it exists

Comping started as a personal tool to stop manually checking eBay sold prices dozens of times during a single sourcing trip — thrift store, garage sale, wholesale lot, whatever. Typing a product name into eBay, filtering to sold listings, and eyeballing a median by hand for every single item is exactly the kind of repetitive check that's easy to skip when you're moving fast, which is usually when it matters most. Comping exists to make that check instant instead of optional.

How the sold-price number is actually calculated

Comping fetches eBay's own sold-listing data for the product you're viewing and takes the median price. That sounds simple, but a plain text search returns more than just matching sales of the same item — it also returns accessories, parts, damaged or "for parts" listings, counterfeits, multi-item lots, and a different bundle or variant of the same product, all mixed in with the real thing.

Rather than assume that's fine, this was actually measured: a systematic audit of 1,000 real products across 13 categories — phones, laptops, audio gear, cameras, gaming hardware, LEGO, trading cards, sneakers, watches, kitchen appliances, tools, and collectibles — checked what was actually skewing the numbers, and by how much.

What the audit found, concretely:

  • Accessories and parts sold separately (a case, a screen protector, a charger) get indexed with the product's own model number, and often outnumber genuine listings enough to drag the median down by an order of magnitude.
  • Listings for the product "for" a different item — a repair part or accessory whose title mentions your product only in passing ("Boot for FUEL Ratchet") — were the single largest source of contamination found.
  • Professionally graded collectibles (PSA, BGS, CGC, SGC) mixed into a raw-card search inflate the median well past what an ungraded copy — what most resellers are actually pricing — sells for. Measured impact: a 70% and a 29% median correction on two real trading-card queries, in both cases downward.
  • New vs. used condition gets blended into one number by default, and the two can differ enormously — an 83% average price gap was measured across products where both conditions had enough comps to compare, and up to 113% on individual phone listings.
  • Sold date matters: where there's enough recent data, weighting toward the last few days of sales instead of a wider historical window keeps the number closer to the current market rather than months-old listings.

Statistical outlier trimming was also tested — median absolute deviation, interquartile range, and simple percentage trimming — and none of it reliably improved accuracy on its own. The contamination doesn't show up as isolated outliers; it shows up as a whole cluster of wrong-but-plausible listings sitting inside the range a naive outlier filter would keep. Removing it required actually identifying *what kind* of listing it was, not just how far its price sat from the middle.

What's still hard

Keyword-based filtering can't catch everything — it can tell a graded card from a raw one, but it can't yet tell two visually similar variants apart with certainty in every case. That's an open problem, not a solved one, and it's stated here rather than glossed over.
